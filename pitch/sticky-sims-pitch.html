02 · THE PROBLEM

Software can teach people risky work safely. It can’t reach them.

Simulation is how you practise the expensive mistake before it costs anything — the lab induction, the safety drill, the machine you only get one chance to break. But the institutions that need it most run locked-down machines that can’t install software, and — increasingly in Europe — aren’t allowed to touch the cloud.

install training-simulator.msi  DENIED — managed device. IT policy.
open cloud-simulator.io  DENIED — pupil data would leave the EU. Legal says no.¹
request procurement review  DEFERRED — no budget line, next cycle.

¹ In July 2022 the Danish data-protection authority banned a municipality’s schools from using Google Workspace over US data transfers (Datatilsynet, Helsingør ruling — gdprhub.eu). Meanwhile 50 million students and educators work on Chromebooks (Google, Sept 2024) — machines built to install nothing. Every simulation vendor answers with an install, an account, or a cloud. The buyer’s machine says no to all three.

The moat isn’t the code. It’s where the code can run.

It runs where vendors can’t follow

No install, no account, no server, no network call. It doesn’t pass IT review — it never triggers one. A school Chromebook, an air-gapped lab, a USB stick in a secure facility: the same file, double-clicked.

09 · DRAGON-PROOFING

The five questions most likely to kill this — asked properly.

They would — if I were competing for their player. I’m not. EA sells installed content to consumers with gaming hardware; its cost base needs exactly the machines and wallets I don’t target. My buyer is an institution whose computers can’t install anything — a place EA has no product for and no economic reason to build one. A 372 KB offline file is beneath their cost structure, not ahead of it.

Krafton’s million first-week copies cut the other way: they prove the genre has demand EA leaves unserved — and inZOI chases the high-end GPU owner, the opposite corner from a school Chromebook. If I ever pitch this as a Sims competitor, kill the pitch. That’s why I don’t.

Today: nobody pays. This is pre-revenue and I won’t dress that up. The buyer I’m going after is — an organisation with a training obligation it already budgets for. Why now: since the 2022 Helsingør ruling, European regulators have been pulling institutions off US clouds while their device fleets stay locked down. The training obligation didn’t go away; the means to meet it did.

The raise exists to convert exactly one such institution into a paying pilot. That pilot is the real answer to this question — and it’s what the money buys.

Not the code — the code is honestly cheap now, for me and for everyone. You’re buying three things. One: the wedge — a working zero-install engine already pointed at buyers other vendors are architecturally locked out of. Two: the verification culture — the swarm shipped confident false claims; the audit caught three of them; the merge gate now catches that class automatically. When code gets cheap, disciplined verification gets more valuable, not less. Three: speed — the same method that produced 9,512 verified lines produces scenario packs.

If AI really does make software nearly free, returns move to whoever ships trustworthy software fastest. That’s the bet.

I don’t beat Labster where Labster works — I sell where they can’t go. They are cloud-delivered by architecture: subscriptions, accounts, servers. Any institution that can use the cloud probably should use them; their $147M raised is my evidence the buyer exists. But air-gapped, offline, data-never-leaves-the-building deployments aren’t a feature they can patch in — they’re an architecture, and a business model, they’d have to abandon.

Those deployments are smaller markets — and I’m one person with a 372 KB payload. My cost floor fits markets their cost floor can’t.

What the money buys is the same three lines as the ask — and one concrete promise: within twelve months there is either a paying pilot, or a documented reason there never will be. I ship verified work or I say plainly that it failed. You’ve seen the habit in this deck: the gaps are marked, not painted over.
